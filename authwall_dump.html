    Snowflake Snowpark -- where have you been all my life? What I used to think: why do I need snowpark? I don't like spark much and all the good parts of snowflake are SQL-first. Nice try, devils but you won't trap me 👹 What I learned: Snowpark isn't useful to me as a spark replacement, its useful as an AWS Lambda replacement 🤯 The need: - I need to {do something data-adjacent with a python script} - this {data thing} could be anything. Could be calling an API to get some weather data (you know I love weather data), could be checking the status of some other thing, could just be me posting an event somewhere (slack notifications anyone?) - honestly, I really only need this stuff to run with my dbt pipeline Today, I actually needed to coordinate an Iceberg metadata refresh with a simple pyiceberg python script to freshen my Tabular product usage data in Snowflake. 1. I installed the KILLER snowflake cli 2. `snow snowpark init` gave me a super simple project skeleton 3. updated my `requirements.txt` with the custom pyiceberg library, so even getting dependencies in Snowflake was easy 4. `snow snowpark build && snow snowpark deploy && snow snowpark execute procedure "mirror_pyiceberg()" That was it! How cool is that? This code now lives happily in my dbt repository. It's a one time set up to define this object, so I keep it with where I keep my other Snowflake infra as code. Now for the cool part -- I can do a few really crazy things in my dbt project: - easiest is just `call mirror_pyiceberg()` on-run-start, simple and classy - little more fancy would be using a dbt python model where I would normally define a stage model and mirror individual tables using the SNOWFLAKE SECRET and my custom snowpark dependency app.zip file in my snowpark stage. - want to get real freaky?     Please stop calling every pipeline a “data product.” I see it a lot, teams proudly calling their dashboards or ETL pipelines “data products.” But a real data product is way more than just moving or storing data. It’s something people can trust, use, and actually make decisions with. It has clear ownership, context, and guarantees for the businesses relying on it. From my experience running enterprise analytics projects, the difference between a pipeline and a true data product is night and day. Here’s what sets it apart: - Purpose drives design: A true data product solves a specific business problem, not just generates numbers. - Context is key: Metadata, lineage, and business meaning make data understandable and actionable. - Accessibility is non-negotiable: It should be discoverable, self-service, and seamlessly integrated into workflows. - Operational guarantees: Reliability, freshness, and consistent behavior are what separate a product from a pipeline. Pipelines are the engine, but the data product is the experience for the user. Without the human-led strategy, governance, and adoption focus, AI or automation alone cannot turn raw data into decision-making power. When was the last time your team built a “data product” that people actually relied on, and not just admired on a slide?

  • David Griffith

    OMERS Private Equity • 3K followers

    I've been focused on problems Field Reimbursement Managers face lately. There is most definitely a tech play to shift FRMs from reactive to proactive. Most FRM teams don’t have a data problem. They have a friction problem: fragmented signals creating delays that extend time to therapy. AI can't (shouldn't?) replace FRM expertise. But it should remove frictions so expertise shows up earlier, more consistently, and at a broader scale. What’s working in the field right now: - Patient 360 that actually updates, unifying hub, SP, EHR, and CRM context so every outreach is informed by the latest status, not last week’s spreadsheet. - No more manual policy diffing. Let models watch policy updates and surface the impacts on active cases and upcoming renewals. - Prioritization that respects reality: rank cases by clinical risk, benefit rules, documentation completeness, and payer cycle timing - not just “oldest first”. - Pre-engagement prep in minutes: generate account-specific briefs with action-ready next steps, coverage nuance, and missing-item checklists. - Smart routing and handoffs: Auto-direct tasks to the right teammate (or the hub) with context preserved, so escalations don’t reset the clock. - Voice-to-CRM that sticks: capture calls and visits into structured fields aligned to workflows, compliantly, scanning for PHI and PQCs. How to implement without boiling the ocean: - Start with one high-friction use case (e.g., renewals or PA resubmits) and measure days-to-therapy and touches-per-case before/after. - Put humans in the loop where policy or clinical nuance drives risk; let models draft, teammates decide. - Integrate to the system of record first; duplicative workflows will stall adoption and trust. - Make transparency a feature: show why a case is prioritized, what data informed it, and where confidence is low. - Treat prompt and taxonomy design like product: consistent language unlocks consistent outcomes across regions and brands. What “good” looks like: fewer rework loops, smoother renewals, fewer “status check” emails, and earlier identification of likely denials before they become denials. The win isn’t flashy AI - it’s dependable access, faster. Curious how others are approaching this: where have AI-driven workflows actually reduced time-to-therapy or touches-per-case, and what surprised you most in the rollout?
